Edge & Corner Whitening

The little white flecks on edges and corners are the most common damage in the hobby — and the most common thing people try to "fix." Here's what whitening really is, why there's no honest repair, and what you can actually do.

PokéSpa · September 1, 2026

Tilt a played card under a lamp and you'll see it: tiny white specks along the edges, a pale scuff on a corner. Collectors call it whitening. It's the single most common flaw on Pokémon cards, and the question we get about it more than any other is the same five words.

"Can you fix the whitening?"

The honest answer is no — and nobody else can either, not honestly. This page explains why, and what your real options are.

What whitening actually is

A trading card is a sandwich. In the middle sits a paper core — and that core is white (on many Pokémon cards it has a telltale dark layer inside, but the outer faces of the stock are pale paper). Printed over it are the ink and gloss layers that carry the card's color and shine.

Whitening is what you see when that outer skin chips or wears away at an edge or corner and the pale core underneath shows through. It isn't a stain, and it isn't something sitting on the card. It's an absence. Material that used to be there is gone.

Almost all of it is friction damage. Shuffling without sleeves. Cards rattling loose in a box or drawer. Pushing a card into a tight sleeve and dragging the edge against the opening. Riding a card in a pocket with keys and lint. Every one of those rubs a few more fibers bare, and the damage only ever accumulates.

Why graders care so much

Corners and edges are two of the four things every major grader scores, alongside surface and centering. In practice, half a card's grade lives on its perimeter — and graders count every fleck. A card can have a clean surface and lovely centering and still come back with a mid grade because the corners are fuzzed white under magnification.

That's exactly why whitening attracts so many bad "solutions." The stakes feel high, the flaw looks small, and a marker seems like such an easy answer. Which brings us to the part of this page that matters most.

The hard truth: there is no fix

Remember what whitening is: missing ink and gloss. There are only two ways to make it disappear. Put new color where the factory's color used to be, or shave the edge back until the wear is gone. The first is recoloring. The second is trimming. Both are card doctoring — not a gray zone, not "restoration with extra steps." Doctoring, full stop.

Edge markers, Sharpies, and "whitening repair pens" are alteration tools. Coloring a card's edge adds ink the factory never put there. PSA labels recolored cards N3 — altered — and won't grade them, and the hobby condemns the practice as universally as it condemns trimming. Anyone selling you a "whitening fix pen" is selling you a tool for doctoring cards. If that card is later sold or submitted as unaltered, the marker didn't fix a flaw. It manufactured a lie — and marker ink rarely matches anyway; under a grader's light it reads instantly as touch-up.

We're a restoration service. It would be convenient for us if a fix existed. It doesn't, and we'd rather tell you that plainly than sell you a tidy-sounding version of doctoring. Where the exact line runs between honest care and alteration — and why we built the whole business on one side of it — is laid out in Cleaning vs. card doctoring.

What you can do honestly

First: stop the clock. Whitening never heals, but it also never has to get worse. Protection is cheap, and the card you sleeve today keeps the corners it has today.

The prevention setup.

Penny sleeve first, then toploader. The soft sleeve shields the surface; the rigid toploader shields the corners. Never a bare card straight into a hard holder — the hard opening chews edges.

Insert top edge first, gently. Pinch the sleeve open so the card slides in without scraping the opening. A tight sleeve forced over a corner causes the very damage it's meant to prevent.

No pocket rides. A loose card in a pocket is a sanding project. If a card travels, it travels sleeved and in a rigid holder — the shop carries what we use ourselves.

Store snug, not loose. Cards that can slide and rattle in a box are quietly wearing their own edges every time the box moves.

Second: make peace with the white. This is the advice nobody selling a pen will give you. A card's condition is part of what you're buying, so buy the condition you can afford and enjoy it as it is. A played copy with honest edges is a real card with a real history. A recolored copy is damaged goods wearing makeup — worth less than the honest version, because now the alteration is the flaw.

And if you're weighing a grading submission, go in with clear eyes: whitening will cost points, no cleaning trick changes that, and a doctored edge risks far worse than a low grade. We've written up how graders actually treat cleaning and preparation in Cleaning cards before grading.

Third: know what a professional can and can't do. A conservative hand can tidy a frayed edge — settling the loose, fuzzed paper fibers so the edge sits clean instead of ragged. That's careful, disclosed work, and it's documented like everything we do. But hear the limit clearly: the white stays. No ink is added, nothing is trimmed, and a restored card is — under every major grader's policy — an altered card, disclosed as such if it's ever sold or graded. We tidy cards because their owners love them, never to chase a grade.

The short version

Whitening is missing material, and missing material has no honest fix. Anything that makes it vanish is doctoring, and graders and collectors treat it exactly that way. What's left to you is real, though: protect the card so the wear stops today, buy condition honestly, and if an edge is frayed enough to bother you, have it tidied conservatively and disclosed. The white flecks aren't shameful. They're just the truth about where a card has been — and in this hobby, the truth is the one thing worth keeping intact.
