Cleaning vs. Card Doctoring: Where the Line Is

Every collector argues about this. We think the line is actually clear — and a restoration service should be the first to draw it out loud.

PokéSpa · Updated August 31, 2026

Wipe the dust off a card and nobody blinks. Trim its edges and you've committed the hobby's cardinal sin. Somewhere between those two acts sits a line, and every collector forum eventually fights about exactly where it runs.

We restore Pokémon cards for a living, so we don't get to be vague about this. Here's where we think the line is, why it's there, and the side of it we've built our whole business on.

The reversibility test

Vintage collecting communities — the long-running vintage Pokémon forums are a good example — have argued this question for years, and a rough consensus has come out of it. It fits in one sentence:

Acceptable card care is reversible, and it adds or removes nothing.

Dust isn't part of the card. Blowing it off changes nothing about the object itself. A humidity warp isn't part of the card either — the paper cupped because of moisture, and letting it relax back flat restores its original shape. But ink, gloss, and edges are the card. Touch those and you're no longer caring for the object. You're manufacturing a different one.

Notice the pattern in the bottom rows. Every condemned practice either adds something, removes something, or exists to hide damage. Collectors don't call that restoration. They call it what it is — the community's own phrase is "akin to fraud" — because when that card is later sold as unaltered, that's exactly what it becomes.

Why the line matters: the Mastro lesson

The most famous card in the world is a doctored card. Bill Mastro, once the biggest auctioneer in the hobby, trimmed the edges of the T206 Honus Wagner — the "Gretzky Wagner" — and concealed it when the card sold. He pleaded guilty to mail fraud and served 20 months in federal prison.

Read that case carefully, because the legal point is the whole point. The crime wasn't scissors touching cardboard. The crime was the concealment at sale — taking a buyer's money for a card represented as something it wasn't. Alteration plus silence equals fraud. That's not a hobby norm; that's federal law.

And the trust penalty doesn't wait for a courtroom. In 2019, collectors surfaced allegations that trimmed and recolored cards tied to a well-known "card doctor" had been resubmitted for grading and sold through major channels. The FBI looked into it. No charges were ever filed, and the man at the center denies altering cards. It didn't matter. The hobby's verdict came anyway: flagged serial numbers, poisoned provenance, years of suspicion hanging over every card that passed nearby. Short of any prosecution, the trust penalty was total. In this hobby, you don't get acquitted in the court that counts.

What other hobbies figured out

Here's the part of this argument that usually gets left out: disclosed conservation is a mainstream, legitimate category in almost every serious collecting hobby.

In comics, CGC's affiliated company CCS performs pressing and dry cleaning openly — it's a published service, not a back-room secret. In coins, NGC runs conservation in-house and frames it as the opposite of doctoring: remove harmful residues so the coin can be assessed honestly, and refuse to re-engrave, re-color, fill, or conceal anything. PCGS Restoration draws the same line. The pattern is identical everywhere: conservation in the open is a service; alteration in the dark is a scandal.

One honest caveat: card grading is stricter than comics or coins. PSA, BGS, and CGC treat even cleaning solutions and pressing — routine in those other hobbies — as alteration. We've broken down exactly what each grader flags in our grading guide. Which leads to the sentence we say more often than any other:

A restored card is, under current grader policy, an altered card. Restore a card because you love it — for display, for play, for preservation. Never to chase a grade. If it's ever graded or sold, the work gets disclosed. Full stop.

The PokéSpa charter

This is the heart of the page, and of the business. It's short on purpose.

What we will never do — or teach.

No trimming. Not a millimeter, not a "clean-up," not ever.

No recoloring or edge touch-ups. We don't add ink a factory didn't.

No filling creases. A crease is the card's history. We won't paper over it.

No gloss enhancement or abrasive buffing. The original surface stays original.

No concealment. Every job is documented with before-and-after photos and a written list of exactly what was done. That record travels with your card.

We'll say no. When the honest answer is that work would hurt the card — or that the card should be left alone — that's the answer you'll get.

Disclose when you sell or grade. Always. We hand you the documentation; passing it on is the deal.

None of this is us being precious. It's the Mastro lesson applied in advance. Disclosure is what keeps restoration on the conservation side of the line — the side NGC and CCS built businesses on — instead of the side that ends careers.

If anyone offers to "improve your grade" with undisclosed work — walk away. That's not restoration and it's not a gray area. It makes you the person selling an altered card as unaltered, and as the hobby's history shows, that can end in a federal courtroom. No grade is worth it.
