Creases, bends & pressing

One of these injuries can come back. The other can't. Here's how to tell a bend from a crease, what pressing really does, and why every major card grader calls a pressed card altered.

PokéSpa · September 1, 2026

A card comes out of a mailer with a corner folded over. Or it lived in a back pocket for an afternoon. Now there's a line across the artwork, and the first question is always the same: can this be fixed?

The honest answer depends on one distinction. Not the size of the mark. Not where it sits. Whether the paper broke.

Bend or crease? They are different injuries

A bend is deformation without breaking. The card curved past flat, but the paper fibers inside stretched and held, and the ink layer on top stayed intact. Bends are often recoverable — the card can relax back toward flat, and once it does, there may be little or no evidence it ever happened.

A crease is a break. The fold went far enough to snap the paper fibers and crack the ink above them. That's why a crease shows as a white line: you're seeing the pale card stock through fractured ink. Broken fibers do not knit back together. Cracked ink does not re-fuse. A crease is permanent, and no amount of flattening will un-break it.

The fingernail test. Hold the card under raking light — a lamp at a low, glancing angle. Draw a fingernail gently across the mark. A crease catches: your nail finds a step where the surface broke. A bend doesn't — the surface is continuous, just curved. Raking light also makes the white line of cracked ink jump out. Thirty seconds, and you know which injury you have.

What pressing actually does

Pressing is geometry, not healing. Sustained, even pressure persuades the card back into a flat plane. For a bend, that's the whole job — the fibers were never broken, so flat is a full recovery.

For a crease, physics has bad news. Pressure can close the valley of the fold. It cannot reconnect snapped fibers or repair cracked ink. A pressed crease lies flat and still shows its white line, plain as ever under raking light. The card looks better on a shelf. The damage is still there, and always will be.

Comics press. Card graders drew the opposite line

If you've spent time in comics, this may feel strange. Pressing is mainstream there — CGC's own affiliated service presses comics before grading, openly. It's an accepted part of that hobby.

Card grading went the other way, firmly. PSA lists crease and wrinkle pressing under its N5 designation — Altered Stock. BGS and CGC Cards treat pressed cards as altered too. There is no major card grader that blesses it.

Pressing is alteration at every card grader. A pressed card is not eligible for a numeric grade at PSA, BGS, or CGC Cards. If you press a card and later sell it, the pressing must be disclosed. That's not our rule — it's theirs, and it's the market's. It's also ours: disclosed restoration, always.

So the calculus is simple. If a card might ever be graded or sold, pressing takes something off the table permanently. Never press a card hoping for a better grade — that path ends at "Altered," or worse, at a buyer who feels deceived.

When flattening honestly makes sense

Plenty of cards are never going near a grading room. Display copies. Binder favorites. Play decks. If you just want a bent card to sit flat in a sleeve or frame, flattening a bend is a reasonable, honest thing to do — the same way you'd flatten a curled card, which is a related story (see warping & curling for the curl-versus-crease distinction and the one fix we bless).

A gentle at-home method — for a bend, not a crease

First, run the fingernail test. If it catches, stop — it's a crease, and pressing will only flatten the geometry around a permanent line. If it's a true bend, patience does the work:

  • Sleeve it. Card in a clean penny sleeve, so nothing touches the surface directly.
  • Sandwich it. Place the sleeved card between smooth interleaving sheets — clean, flat, non-textured paper on both sides.
  • Weight it. Under even, flat weight. A stack of heavy books works. Even pressure across the whole card, never a point load.
  • Wait. Days, not hours. Check every day or two. Fibers relax slowly, and rushing is how bends become creases.
  • Mind the room. Around 45–55% relative humidity. Too dry and the stock is brittle; too damp invites other problems.

And the hard nevers: never heat, never an iron, never a laminator. Heat softens adhesives, blisters holofoil, and glosses surfaces in ways that read instantly as damage — or as doctoring. Time and weight, nothing else.

Purpose-built presses

Purpose-built card presses and clamp setups exist, and for someone flattening bends regularly they beat a book stack — steadier pressure, better plates. We keep the bench tools we actually trust on our shop page. The same rules travel with the tool: a press cannot fix a crease, and a pressed card follows the same disclosure rules as any other.

The bottom line

Learn the fingernail test and you'll never have to guess. A bend can come home; give it a sleeve, flat weight, and a week. A crease is a scar the card keeps — pressing hides its shape, never its line, and every card grader will call the result altered. Flatten for the shelf and the deck box, disclose if it ever sells, and never press for a grade.
