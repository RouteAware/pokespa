Indents, Dings & Lifted Edges

A tiny dent in your card is not a death sentence. Here's how to tell a recoverable indent from a permanent ding, and why a lifted edge is a different animal entirely.

PokéSpa · September 1, 2026

You tilt the card under a lamp and there it is. A little depression in the surface. Your stomach drops.

Take a breath. Of all the ways Pokémon cards get hurt, this category carries the most genuine hope — and the most confusion. Three very different things get called "a dent." One of them often recovers. One never does. One was never damage at all. Let's sort them out.

Indent, ding, or print line?

An indent is compressed fibers with the ink still intact. Something pressed into the card without breaking the surface. A fingernail. A grain of grit trapped under a stack. The classic toploader dent, where an over-stuffed box pushed the hard plastic into the card face. The paper is squeezed, but nothing tore and no ink cracked.

A ding is an impact that broke the ink or the surface layer. You'll see white where color used to be, or a tiny crackle in the finish. The card didn't just compress — something fractured.

A print line is neither. Factory print lines and roller marks come off the press with the card. They run straight and parallel, and they sit under the surface rather than pressed into it. They're a manufacturing quirk, not damage, and no restoration touches them.

That distinction — compressed versus broken — is the whole diagnosis. Everything else follows from it.

How to diagnose it at home

Two tools, five minutes.

The raking-light test. Angle a lamp low across the card, almost parallel to the surface, and slowly rotate the card. This "raking light" throws a shadow into every depression, no matter how shallow. It's how conservators inspect paintings, and it works just as well on a trading card. An indent you could barely feel becomes obvious. If nothing shows under raking light from any angle, you're likely looking at a print line, not a dent.

Then bring in a loupe. Magnification answers the one question raking light can't: is the ink broken? Look directly at the low spot. Unbroken color that simply dips and comes back up means an indent. White flecks, hairline cracks, or missing ink mean a ding. A good loupe and a small inspection lamp are both in our shop, and they're the same tools we reach for first at the bench.

The honest hope ranking

Here's the spine of this article, and it's good news for once:

  • Indents are the most recoverable damage category we see. Compressed-but-unbroken fibers hold a memory of their old shape. With stable humidity and gentle, even, flat weight — applied over days or weeks, not hours — they sometimes relax substantially. It's the same slow, reversible method we use for de-warping a curled card: patience and physics, nothing added, nothing removed.
  • Dings with broken ink are permanent. Paper fibers can relax. Cracked ink cannot heal, and lost ink cannot honestly come back. Anything that "fixes" a ding is repainting, and that's a line we document loudly when we cross it — and refuse to cross quietly.
  • Print lines don't need hope. They're original to the card. Leave them be.

Two honesty notes before you get excited. "Sometimes relax substantially" is not "disappears" — deep indents usually improve rather than vanish. And recovery never changes what a card is worth to a grader on the day it was damaged; we improve how a card looks and survives, and we never promise a grade. Any card that has been worked on is an altered card under every major grader's policy, which is why disclosure is the whole foundation of what we do.

One firm rule while you wait: never push an indent back out from behind. A thumb or a burnishing tool from the back side bends fibers past their limit, and now you have a crease — broken fibers, a permanently worse card, and a much harder conversation about pressing. Flat, even, gentle, slow. That's the entire method.

Lifted edges: when a card starts coming apart

Modern Pokémon cards are laminated sandwiches — layers of paper and foil bonded at the factory. A lifted edge or peeling corner means those layers are separating. This is delamination, and it's not an indent problem. It's a structural one, and it gets worse with handling.

Please don't reach for the glue. Super glue, white glue, glue sticks — every DIY adhesive we've seen on a delaminated card ruined it permanently. Glue wicks between layers, stains, hardens the wrong areas, and telegraphs through the surface. There is no undo. A lifting edge is bad; a glued edge is finished.

Professional stabilization with conservation-appropriate adhesive does exist, and let's be plain about what it is: restoration. It's legitimate only when it's documented and disclosed, and a stabilized card is an altered card at any grader — full stop. What stabilization buys you is a card that stops falling apart, done by careful hands, with paperwork that tells the truth. If an edge on a card you love is lifting, that's our service work, not a weekend project. Our DIY-or-pro triage walks the whole decision honestly.

Prevention costs almost nothing

  • Don't overstuff. Boxes and binders under pressure are where toploader dents and stack indents are born. Leave room.
  • Blow out sleeves first. One grain of grit inside a sleeve becomes a pressed-in indent under any weight. A quick puff from a blower before the card goes in costs two seconds.
  • Insert toploaders with care. Sleeve first, then slide straight in — a snagged corner is how edges start lifting in the first place.

An indent is often the beginning of a recovery story. A ding is a card's history, worn honestly. A lifted edge is a call for help. Knowing which one you're holding is most of the battle — and if you'd rather not guess, that's what we're here for.
