Holo scratches: what they are, what lifts, and what doesn't

Half of what looks like holo scratching is just grime — and it wipes right off. The other half is damage to the foil itself. Here's how to tell the difference, why "scratch removers" are a trap, and how to stop the next scratch before it happens.

PokéSpa · September 1, 2026

What a holo scratch actually is

A holo card gets its shimmer from a thin foil layer under the printed art. That foil is delicate. A holo scratch lives in or under the surface over that layer — a tiny groove cut through the finish that scatters light instead of reflecting it. That's why scratches flash white when you tilt the card.

How do they get there? The number one cause, by a wide margin: dry-wiping. A speck of grit sits on the card. A cloth or a thumb drags it across the surface. That speck acts like a plow. One careless wipe can leave a scratch that outlasts everything else on the card.

The second cause is debris inside sleeves. Dust and grit work their way into a sleeve, and then every insertion and removal drags the card across it. A dirty sleeve doesn't protect a card. It sands it.

And sometimes the factory did it. Holo cards can leave the printer with fine lines already in the foil. That matters, because a factory line and a scratch are different problems — and different conversations with a grader.

Scratch, print line, or just grime?

Before you mourn a card, diagnose it. You need three things: an air blower, a strong light held at a low angle (raking light), and a 10x loupe. The blower and loupe both live in our shop, and they're the same tools we reach for first at the bench.

Blow the surface clean. Then hold the card under raking light and tilt it slowly. Real scratches jump out as bright lines that move with the light. Now look closer with the loupe and ask two questions.

Is it a factory print line? Print lines are usually straight, parallel, and sit under the surface — the finish above them is smooth and unbroken. Scratches are random. They cross each other at odd angles, they wander, and they sit in the surface where a fingernail edge or raking light will catch them.

Is it even a scratch at all? This is where a lot of "scratched" cards get good news.

The wipe-off test. Blow the card clean first — always. Then make one gentle pass with a clean, dry microfiber cloth. Residue, grime, and surface haze lift and disappear. A true scratch stays exactly where it was. If the line survives a clean microfiber pass under raking light, it's in the foil, not on it.

We see this constantly: a card written off as "scratched to death" that's really wearing a film of old sleeve residue. That lifts. It's the best outcome there is, and it's worth checking for before anything else.

The hard truth about "scratch removers"

Search for a fix and you'll find polishes, compounds, and "scratch remover" pens promising to make holo scratches vanish. We have to be straight with you, because this is the part of the hobby where honest and dishonest part ways.

A real holo scratch is missing material. The groove is cut. Nothing pours the foil's finish back into it. What polishes actually do is fill the groove with product and lay new gloss over the surface. The scratch isn't gone — it's buried under something that was never part of the card.

Polish is alteration. Every major grader treats filled or polished surfaces as an altered card. PSA grades it N5 — "Altered Stock" — and PSA has decertified cards found to have been treated with cleaning and polish products after grading. There is no polish, compound, or pen that removes a holo scratch in a way graders accept. If a polished card is ever sold, the work must be disclosed to the buyer. That's not our house rule — that's the line between restoration and doctoring.

So can anything be done? Honestly: surface grime and residue can be lifted, and that alone transforms how many cards look. The scratch underneath is permanent. A collector who loves a card can choose a disclosed cosmetic treatment for display — eyes open, papers honest, never headed to a grader or an undisclosed sale. We wrote more about exactly where that line sits in cleaning vs. card doctoring.

What we will never tell you is that treatment improves a grade. It doesn't. Per grader policy, a restored card is an altered card.

Prevention is the whole game

Because removal isn't honestly possible, everything worth doing happens before the scratch. Three habits cover almost all of it.

Blow before you wipe. Every time. Air first, cloth second. A blower lifts the grit that a wipe would drag. This is the grit-first rule, and it's the backbone of our whole safe cleaning guide. A dry wipe over an unblown card is the single most common way a clean card becomes a scratched one.

Keep sleeves clean. A gritty sleeve is sandpaper you use twice a day. If a sleeve has visible dust inside, or it's been through years of shuffling, retire it. Sleeves are the cheapest part of this hobby. Foil isn't replaceable.

One card per sleeve. Two cards in one sleeve grind against each other with every handle. It feels efficient. It's mutual sanding.

If a card is headed to a grader, the stakes double — read cleaning cards before grading before you touch it at all.

The short version

Blow it clean. Check it under raking light. Wipe-test with clean microfiber — if the line lifts, it was grime and you just fixed it. If it stays, it's a scratch: permanent, and not honestly removable by any polish on the market. Then protect the card so the next one never happens. That's the whole craft.
